Publish prices when most deals land on a standard plan, show a starting price when deals vary but have a floor, and explain cost drivers when every deal is custom. Whichever you choose, a B2B buyer should be able to judge fit and budget without a call. This guide covers the decision for B2B SaaS teams, as of October 2026.

## What are the main ways to handle a B2B SaaS pricing page?

There are four common models, and many SaaS companies mix two of them. The table shows each with a public pricing page that used it on October 18, 2026.

Table: Four SaaS pricing page models with public examples (checked October 18, 2026)

| Model | What the page shows | Public example | Fits when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Published tiers | Plan names, per-user prices, a free plan (a freemium model), and a contact-sales top tier | Slack: Free, Pro, and Business+ with prices; Enterprise+ says "Contact sales for pricing" | Most customers buy a standard plan |
| Starting price | "Starts at" prices per tier, a monthly or annual toggle, and talk-to-sales buttons | HubSpot Marketing Hub: "Starts at" prices, plus cost drivers such as contacts and seats, and a disclosed onboarding fee | Deals vary, but every deal has a floor |
| Usage-based pricing | Unit rates, included usage, overages, and spend controls | Datadog: per-host and per-GB rates, annual or on-demand; Vercel: a base fee with included usage credit and spend limits | Cost scales with consumption |
| Quote with cost drivers | No numbers, but how pricing works and what moves it | Gong: "Licenses are priced per user" and a platform fee "based on the number of users supported" | Every deal is configured |

Two details from those pages are worth copying whatever model you choose. HubSpot states the extra fee that isn't in the headline price: the cost shown "does not include the required, one-time Professional Onboarding". Vercel explains spend controls in its FAQ, so a buyer knows a usage bill can be capped. And security vendors don't always hide prices: CrowdStrike publishes per-device prices for its small-business bundles and keeps "Contact sales" for the managed tier.

## How do you decide whether to publish prices?

Answer five questions about how you sell, then pick the model that matches your pricing strategy. The pricing page should reflect how you actually sell, not how you'd like to.

1. **How much do deals vary?** If most customers pay a list price, publish it. If the price depends on seats, usage, or modules, show the starting point and the drivers.
2. **Who buys?** A single team lead buying with a credit card needs a number. A buying committee needs the number plus terms, limits, and security details.
3. **Does sales need discovery to price?** If the honest answer is yes, say so and explain what discovery covers. If it's only a habit, a starting price is better.
4. **Can you state a floor?** Even a custom-priced product usually has a minimum. "Plans start at" with a clear unit (per seat, per month, billed annually) answers the first budget question.
5. **What do buyers already see elsewhere?** If competitors publish prices, a page without numbers can make your product look more expensive to potential customers than it is.

Table: Matching the pricing page model to how you sell

| How you sell | Pricing page model |
| --- | --- |
| Self-serve sign-up, card payment, standard plans | Published tiers with a free plan or trial |
| Sales-assisted, seat-based, discounts by volume | Starting price per tier and a quote route for larger teams |
| Billed on consumption | Unit rates, included usage, overage rates, and spend controls |
| Configured enterprise deals with implementation | Cost drivers, typical contract terms, and a fast quote route |

## What makes a SaaS pricing page design easy to compare?

One value metric, plans a buyer can compare side by side, and answers to billing questions on the same page. These habits apply whichever model you pick:

- **Charge by one value metric.** The value metric is the unit the price scales with, such as seats, hosts, contacts, or usage. Use the same one across plans and show it next to every price.
- **Name plans for who they're for.** A tier name that describes the buyer, such as a team or enterprise plan, helps a visitor find their place before reading features.
- **Make each pricing tier visually distinct.** Keep the number of pricing plans small enough to compare at a glance, and highlight one recommended plan if most customers should start there.
- **Lead with outcomes, then list features.** Give each plan a one-line value proposition that says what it lets a team do, and put the full list of different features in a detailed plan comparison table below.
- **Show the billing period.** If annual and monthly pricing options differ, use a clear toggle and say which one the page shows by default.
- **State trial terms.** If the trial needs no card, say "no credit card required"; if it does, say that too.
- **Put real proof near the plans.** Customer logos, a short testimonial, or security certifications are useful social proof at the point of decision, as long as they're real and current.
- **Answer objections in an FAQ.** Billing, refunds, data privacy, cancellation, and what happens when a team outgrows its plan.

## What should a pricing page include if you don't publish prices?

Everything except the final number. "Contact sales" on its own answers nothing; a page that explains how pricing works answers most of the first call.

- **The value metric.** Per user, per device, per account, per usage unit, or a platform fee plus seats, as Gong explains.
- **The cost drivers.** The three or four things that move the price, such as team size, modules, contract length, or data volume.
- **What's included.** Support level, onboarding, integrations, and security features at each level.
- **Fees outside the headline price.** Onboarding, implementation, or required add-ons, stated plainly as HubSpot does.
- **Contract terms.** Minimum term, billing frequency, and how renewal works.
- **The quote process.** What you need from the buyer, and how quickly they'll hear back.

A short "What does [product] cost?" FAQ with a plain answer, even if it's a range or a starting point, gives buyers and search engines a quotable sentence.

## What does a buying committee look for on a pricing page?

More than the headline number. Forrester's 2026 buying study says the typical buying decision now includes 13 internal stakeholders and nine external influencers, and Gartner reported in March 2026 that 67% of B2B buyers prefer a rep-free experience. Many of those people will judge your pricing page without ever talking to sales.

Table: What each person on a buying committee checks

| Role | What they look for |
| --- | --- |
| The team lead who found you | Which plan fits, what the trial includes |
| Finance | Billing unit, annual versus monthly price, discounts, invoicing |
| Procurement | Contract length, renewal and cancellation terms, order forms |
| Security and IT | SSO, data residency, compliance reports, which plan includes them |
| Executive sponsor | Total cost at the planned team size, what it replaces, and the case for the return |

A plan comparison table that shows which security and admin features sit in which pricing tier saves several emails. So does a line on how renewals work and how to cancel.

## How do AI assistants and search engines use your pricing page?

They can only repeat what's published. In the same March 2026 survey, Gartner found that 45% of B2B buyers used AI during a recent purchase. When a buyer asks an AI assistant what your product costs, the answer comes from your pricing page if it's clear, or from review sites, old articles, and guesses if it isn't.

Three practical rules follow:

1. **Put prices and pricing explanations in the HTML.** Prices that appear on your website only after a script runs or a selector is changed may not be in the version crawlers read.
2. **Write one plain sentence per plan.** "Pro costs $X per user per month, billed annually" is easy to quote correctly; a price split across styled elements is not.
3. **Use structured data only where it fits.** Google's SoftwareApplication rich results require a price and either an aggregate rating or a review, so many B2B SaaS pricing pages aren't eligible. Markup must match what's on the page.

Keep the page current. An old price on your own site is the most authoritative wrong answer an AI assistant can find.

## Do click-to-cancel rules apply to B2B pricing pages?

Not the 2024 federal rule, which no longer stands. The FTC's 2024 amended Negative Option Rule covered business-to-business transactions as well as consumer ones, but the Eighth Circuit vacated it on July 8, 2025, which reinstated the 1973 version. In March 2026, the FTC published an advance notice of proposed rulemaking, and as of October 18, 2026, no replacement rule is final.

That doesn't make renewal terms a free-for-all. Other federal laws and state automatic-renewal laws can still apply, and they vary by state. Practical habits that hold up in any case: show the billing period and renewal terms next to the price, state how to cancel, and match the pricing page to the order form. Check the details with counsel; this isn't legal advice.

## How do you test a pricing page change?

Measure what the page is for: qualified conversations, not just clicks. In Google Analytics or your product analytics, track the conversion rate from pricing page views to demo requests and sign-ups, ask the sales team whether first calls still spend time on price, and watch for changes in deal size and sales cycle. Many B2B SaaS pricing pages don't get enough traffic for a fast A/B test, so change one thing at a time and compare a few weeks before and after.
