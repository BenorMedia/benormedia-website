Buyers verify what they read, and a B2B case study is proof they can check. One that sales will use shows a buyer like the prospect, a measured result, and written approval from the client. This guide, as of October 2026, gives B2B marketing and sales teams the template, the client checklist, and the FTC rules.

## What is a B2B case study, and why do buyers rely on it?

A B2B case study is a customer story that shows how one business used your product or service as the solution to a specific problem, with results the client has approved. It's written for the next buyer, so it names the problem, the change, and the proof in terms a similar company would recognize. Some teams call the same thing a customer success story. The best ones read like a short project report, not an advertisement, and as success stories pile up they shape brand perception too, because they show which companies already trust you.

Buyers lean on them because business purchases are group decisions. Forrester's 2026 State of Business Buying report says the typical buying decision involves 13 internal stakeholders and nine external influencers, and that buyers respond to their mistrust of generative AI answers by looking to trusted sources for validation. Each of those decision makers and influencers has questions, and not all of them will ever speak to a rep.

Gartner's May 2026 survey of 645 B2B buyers found that 45% had used generative AI in a recent purchase, mainly to gather information on vendors and products, and that 69% prefer to validate AI-generated insights with sales reps. Buyers were nearly split on which source misleads them more: 51% said they were more likely to encounter misleading information from generative AI, and 49% said from a sales rep.

Review platforms show the same habit. TrustRadius, which runs a review platform and so has a stake in the answer, surveyed 1,862 technology buyers in January 2026. It found that 74% use reviews in purchase decisions, and that 94% of the buyers who used AI said they fact-check its answers at least sometimes.

A case study is where those checks land. A buyer, or the rep a buyer asks, can point to a specific, attributable story about the value a client received, instead of a claim your brand makes about itself on a landing page. For the marketing team, the same story keeps working in content marketing, because posts, newsletters, sales emails, campaigns, and webinar examples can all draw on one approved account of what happened. In B2B marketing, that makes one good case study a reusable part of your content strategy, not a one-off project. Our post on [what B2B buyers need from your website](/blog/what-b2b-buyers-need-from-your-website) covers the wider set of proof that buyers look for.

## What are the key elements of a B2B case study?

A B2B case study has ten key elements, shown in the table below, and together they make a structure a buyer can scan in a minute and verify in five. Keep the client as the hero and your product as the help they chose, and follow the same narrative every time: the problem, the solution, and the measured outcome it delivered. Focus each story on one target audience, meaning the industry, company size, and problem of the deals you want more of.

Table: A B2B case study template, section by section

| Section | What it says | Proof to include |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Headline | The result, in the client's terms | One number with its time period |
| At a glance | Company type, size, industry, region, business model, product or technology used, and project length | Facts the client has approved |
| The situation | The client's primary business problem, such as slow manual processes or lost deals, and what it cost | A baseline metric and how it was measured |
| Why they chose you | The alternatives considered and the deciding factor | The client's own words |
| What was done | The solution, scope, timeline, the client's internal team and yours, integrations with back end systems and other technology, and how it fits existing workflows and how users adopted it | Dates, milestones, and final deliverables |
| Results | Financial, operational, or strategic impact: what changed, by how much, over what period | Baseline, result, period, and source for each metric |
| Key takeaways | Two or three lessons another team could apply | The client's agreement that they're fair |
| Quote | One or two lines from a named person | Name, title, and written approval |
| What's next | How the work continues | Optional |
| Next step | One clear call to action, such as a link to talk to sales or see the product | A single action |

Put numbers in context. Impressive results and significant improvements mean little without a baseline: "demo requests up 40%" doesn't say what the starting point was, over what period, or where the number came from. If sales are seasonal, compare with the same period in the previous year. Give every story the same kind of result line: what rose or fell, from which starting figure to which ending figure, between which dates, and in which system the data was measured.

Cover the kind of impact each reader cares about: financial, such as revenue growth, lower cost, customer acquisition cost, or payback; operational, such as hours saved or fewer manual processes; or strategic, such as a new market or new opportunities in a segment. If you can't get a number, describe the change in operational terms the client confirms. The best examples are concrete, such as "the sales team now sends one link instead of three PDFs".

Two sections do most of the work. The situation has to define the client's primary business problem, including any market dynamics that forced the decision, because without it the results have nothing to be measured against. The takeaways say what another team in a similar position could apply, which is what makes the story useful to someone who isn't your client. For the quote, use the client's actual words, lightly cut for length, with the speaker's name and title. A named quote can enhance credibility; a line from "a marketing director" with no name reads as invented.

## Which types of case study should you write?

Start with customer success stories, because they answer the question every buyer asks: has this worked for a company like mine? Other types fill gaps in the sales process and the customer experience.

Table: Types of case study and what each proves

| Type | Best for | Proof it needs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Customer success story | Showing results for one segment or use case | Before-and-after metrics and a named quote |
| Implementation story | Easing IT, security, and procurement concerns about a new platform or a new site | Timeline, integrations, and the outcome of the security review |
| Campaign or growth case study | Marketing results from one campaign, such as a co-marketing campaign with partners | Channel metrics with period and source |
| Partner story | Integration and channel partners | A joint customer outcome and quotes from both sides |
| Video testimonial | Champions and executives who won't read a page | On-screen name and title, plus a transcript on your own page, not only on a video platform |

Choose the type by the objection you need to address, because your sales strategy decides which objections matter most. If deals stall in security review, an implementation story does more than another results story. If a growth-stage prospect says they're too small for your solution, a story about a client of that size beats a famous logo. Partner stories need extra care, because both partners have to approve the same joint customer outcome. Focus the first stories on the target audience segments where you want more deals, as set by your go-to-market strategy.

## How do you collect the material for a case study?

Ask for the data before you write, and for the permissions before you design the page. Start with the clients most likely to say yes: your customer success team manages the relationships and knows who renewed, expanded, or spoke well of the product unprompted, and your CRM data shows which segments you win most often and where growth matters most. Six things make a complete case study:

1. **Baseline and result.** The metric before, the metric after, the dates, and the system it came from, such as the CRM, an analytics platform, or a billing system.
2. **The decision story.** What triggered the project, such as a change in market dynamics or a failed tool, who was involved, which other solutions the client looked at, and why they chose you.
3. **Implementation facts.** How long it took, who did what on the client's internal team and on yours, what was delivered, how end users adopted it, and what was harder than expected.
4. **A named quote.** One person, with the title they want shown.
5. **Permission scope.** Written approval for each element: company and brand names, logo, a person's name and photo, quote, and each metric.
6. **Review contacts.** Who in marketing, communications, or legal needs to approve the final version.

One interview with the project owner can cover the first four. Send the questions in advance so they can pull the numbers, and ask which system each number came from so you can cite it. Focus the conversation on decisions and numbers, not praise, and create a checklist from the six asks so the process repeats for every client. Examples beat opinions, so ask the client to walk through a typical week before the project. Open questions work best: what was happening before you looked for a solution, what else did you consider, what changed once it was live, and what would you tell a company in your position?

## How do you get client approval without stalling the project?

Make it easy to say yes, and give one person on your B2B marketing team ownership of the process from interview to sign-off. A story stuck in a client's legal review helps nobody, and a pushy request puts the relationship at risk, so the process matters as much as the draft.

- **Agree the scope early.** Ask for permission when the project ends well, not months later, and confirm in writing what you may publish. Check the customer contract too, because it may restrict use of the client's name, logo, and brand guidelines. Ask for the logo files and brand usage rules at the same time, so the page shows the client's brand correctly.
- **Send one draft, with the facts flagged.** Highlight every number and quote so the reviewer checks those first, and send the draft to any partners named in the story.
- **Offer an unnamed version.** If the client can't be named, describe the company by type, size, and industry, such as "a mid-market cybersecurity company in Europe", with details they approve. It carries less weight than a named story, so ask for a named version first.
- **Never name a client without written permission.** That includes the client's brand name and logo on the page, in sales decks, and in proposals.
- **Set a review date.** Companies get acquired, change their brand, and switch vendors, so recheck each story once a year and update or retire the ones that no longer fit.

## What do FTC rules say about case study results?

They can apply to B2B case studies, which are marketing assets built on a client's endorsement. The FTC's Endorsement Guides, codified in 16 CFR Part 255, define "product" to include services, brands, companies, and industries, so results for a service or a software solution are covered. Three rules matter most for case studies:

1. **Typical results.** If you don't have proof that a featured result is what customers generally achieve, the advertisement must clearly disclose the generally expected performance.
2. **Material connections.** A connection that could affect how readers weigh the endorsement, and that they wouldn't expect, must be disclosed. The Guides list payment and free or discounted products as examples, so a client who received a discount for taking part could fit.
3. **No distorting edits.** An endorsement may not be reworded or taken out of context in a way that distorts the endorser's opinion or experience.

The Guides speak mostly of consumers and have no section on business buyers, so how strictly each rule reaches a B2B audience is a question for counsel. Following them anyway costs little and protects trust.

The FTC also has a separate rule on consumer reviews and testimonials, in effect since October 21, 2024. It prohibits fake or false consumer reviews and testimonials, including ones that misrepresent the writer's experience with a product or service, and the FTC says a business that posts testimonials on its own website is disseminating them, not merely hosting them. The FTC adds that the rule's definitions of testimonials don't include endorsements by businesses or other entities, so whether a quote from a named person at a client company counts is another question for counsel. This isn't legal advice.

## How do you turn one case study into sales enablement and content marketing assets?

Write the full story once, then create a version for each of the decision makers and influencers on the buying committee, with the solution and the results it delivered explained in that role's terms.

Table: What each role looks for in a case study

| Role | What they want from the story | Best format |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Champion or end user | How daily work and the customer experience changed for end users | Full web page and a short video clip |
| Finance | Cost, payback, and how the result was measured | One-page summary with the numbers |
| IT and security | Integrations, data handling, and the security review | A paragraph or appendix on implementation |
| Executive sponsor | The business value and the risk avoided | Headline, at-a-glance box, and quote |
| Procurement | Timeline and how the engagement was set up | At-a-glance box |

Then make the stories easy for sales to find. Tag each one by industry, company size, use case (a digital transformation project, say), and the objection it answers, such as "we tried this before" or "we're too small for this". A rep looking for proof of value for a fintech prospect worried about migration risk should find the right story in one search, in the sales enablement platform and the other tools your sales team already uses. Personalized versions only help if reps can find them. If your marketing strategy uses intent data or account engagement signals, let them choose the story: an account reading about migration gets the migration story.

Make it a shared job between B2B marketing and sales, and treat the library as part of your sales strategy, not an archive. When marketing and sales share one process and one library, reps find proof sooner in the sales cycle, and marketing learns which stories move deals. The marketing team writes and maintains the stories, and the sales team says which objections still need one. Each quarter, review which stories reps actually send and which campaigns use them, and retire the ones that no longer match what you sell. A single approved interview also saves content production work for the marketing team, because the same facts can feed your content marketing: the web page, a one-page summary, a quote for the pricing page, and a snippet for sales emails and campaigns.

## How do you make a case study page easy to find and cite?

Publish each story as a page on your website, with the facts in the text, not only as a PDF or an image. That way it supports your SEO strategy as well as your sales work.

- **Keep the proof in HTML.** Numbers, dates, names, and roles in plain text can be read by search engines and quoted correctly by AI assistants and other platforms that read the page. A figure that exists only inside an image can't be read as text.
- **Write for skeptical readers.** Google's guidance on helpful content says trust is the most important aspect of E-E-A-T, and it asks whether content provides original information, reporting, research, or analysis. A case study is original information, if the facts hold. Google also asks who created the content and how, so name the author and say how the results were measured.
- **Use one template for every story.** The same structure each time lets SEO practices scale: one H1, a descriptive title, internal links from the product pages each story supports, and Article markup. Google's Article structured data has no required properties, and it recommends properties such as the headline, the publication and modification dates, and the author's name and page.
- **Don't mark up testimonials as reviews to chase stars.** Google says that when the business being reviewed controls the reviews about itself, its pages using LocalBusiness or any other Organization structured data are ineligible for the star review feature.
- **Skip special AI markup.** Google's documentation on AI features says there are no additional requirements to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode: a page has to be indexed and eligible to show in Google Search with a snippet.
- **Give stories a home.** Create a /customers/ section on your site with one page per story, linked from the product and solution pages each story supports, so the traffic those pages already get reaches the proof at the point of decision. Put the result and the type of client in the page title and first paragraph, so the page makes sense in a search result, in a rep's email, and in an AI summary. Add a link to the full library, so readers can explore stories from clients like them.

## How do you measure whether case studies work?

Measure use, engagement, and influence, and treat each as a signal, not proof. A case study is one touchpoint among several, so a last-click report on web traffic will undercount it.

Table: Ways to measure case study performance

| Signal | Where the data comes from | What it tells you | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sales usage | A sales enablement platform, email tracking, or a CRM field where reps log the story they sent | Which stories reps trust and which they ignore | Depends on reps logging it |
| Page engagement | Web analytics tools: traffic to the page, scroll depth, time on page, and clicks to the next step | Whether visitors read the proof | Engagement isn't intent |
| Conversion from the page | Demo requests or form submissions that start on a case study page, or arrive from campaigns that link to it | Whether the call to action works | Many buyers read, leave, and return by another route |
| Deal influence | The CRM or marketing platform: opportunities where a contact viewed or received a case study | Which stories appear in deals that progress | Correlation, not cause |
| Self-reported source | A "how did you hear about us" field on the form | Whether buyers mention your brand or customer stories in their own words | Buyers remember some touchpoints better than others |

Our post on [attribution without an attribution tool](/blog/b2b-marketing-attribution) shows how UTMs, self-reported source, and CRM fields can work together. Review the signals each quarter next to sales feedback, and ask reps one more question: which story did you wish you had last month?

Start small. B2B marketing teams don't need twenty stories to begin: pick the client with the clearest result, run the interview with the six asks above, and create one page with the template. Then see which story reps send first, and add stories by segment and objection from there.
