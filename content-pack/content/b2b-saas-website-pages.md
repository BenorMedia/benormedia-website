A B2B SaaS website needs a home page, product and use-case pages, pricing, proof, security and trust information, content for buyers who are still researching, and a clear path to a demo or contact. Build first the pages a buyer needs to decide, then add the rest as your team can maintain them.

## What does a B2B SaaS website have to do?

A B2B SaaS website has four jobs: explain the product, build trust, start a sales conversation, and stay easy for marketing to change.

1. **Explain the product to a buyer who researches first.** [Gartner's survey of 646 B2B buyers](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-03-09-gartner-sales-survey-finds-67-percent-of-b2b-buyers-prefer-a-rep-free-experience), published in March 2026, found that 67% prefer a rep-free experience. A buyer who skips the sales call reads the site for the answers a salesperson would give.
2. **Build trust before the first call.** A buyer who cannot ask a peer needs proof on the page: named customers, approved quotes, and clear answers on security and data handling.
3. **Start a conversation.** Each page should point to one next step, such as a demo request or a trial, and that step should reach your CRM.
4. **Stay easy to change.** A SaaS product keeps changing, so a site only a developer can edit falls behind it.

## Which pages does a B2B SaaS website need?

A B2B SaaS website needs nine kinds of page, and each earns its place by answering a question a buyer asks before a sales call.

Table: Pages a B2B SaaS website needs, what each is for, and what it should hold

| Page | What it is for | What it should hold |
|---|---|---|
| Home | Say who the product is for and where to go next | The problem you solve, one line of proof, and one primary action |
| Product and use-case pages | Answer "does this solve my problem?" for each role or use case | How the product handles the problem, screenshots, proof, and a next step |
| Pricing | Let a buyer judge fit and budget before a call | Plans, or how pricing works, and how to ask for a quote |
| Integrations | Show that the product works with the buyer's tools | An overview page and one page per integration |
| Customers and proof | Reduce risk for a buyer with no peer reference | Named customers, approved quotes, and results the customer agreed to publish |
| Security and trust | Answer the questions IT, security, and procurement ask | How data is handled, and only the certifications your company holds |
| Resources | Reach buyers who are researching, not yet ready to talk | Guides and how-tos, each answering one question |
| Comparison and alternatives | Help a buyer who is choosing between options | A fair side-by-side of checkable facts, including where each option wins |
| Demo or contact | Turn intent into a conversation | A short form, what happens next, and a connection to your CRM |

For the trust questions of a specific industry, see [fintech web design](page:fintech-web-design), [cybersecurity web design](page:cybersecurity-web-design) and [HR tech web design](page:hr-tech-web-design). ## What makes a B2B SaaS website easy for marketing to run?

A B2B SaaS website is easy for marketing to run when the design system, the CMS content, and the access roles are kept separate, so each person can change what they own and nothing else.

In Webflow's documentation, **components** are reusable blocks of structure, styles, and interactions. When the main component changes, every instance updates, and properties let each instance carry its own text, images or links. **Variables** store style values such as colors, fonts and sizes, and a change reaches everything that uses them.

**CMS Collections** hold content with a shared structure. A Collection is a database for one type of content, each item is an entry, and Webflow generates a Collection page for every item. Reference fields link items across Collections, so an integration page can point to the use cases it supports.

**Roles** decide who can change what. Webflow describes three of them as follows (as of October 2026).

Table: Webflow roles and the work each one suits on a SaaS site

| Role | What Webflow says the role does | Example task on a SaaS site |
|---|---|---|
| Designer | Works with every design feature | Change a layout or build a new component |
| Marketer | Builds and edits pages from components | Assemble a campaign landing page from approved components |
| Content editor | Edits content only, such as text, images, and Collection items | Fix a headline or add a customer story to the CMS |

A content editor can publish where permissions allow, but cannot change the design, edit components, or create Collections, so an update that needs a new layout calls for a designer.

Webflow announced that its legacy Editor would no longer be available from August 4, 2026, and named the content editor role as the closest match, so guides that still describe the legacy Editor are out of date. Roles depend on seat types, so confirm which seats your Webflow plan includes before launch.

## How do SEO and AI search fit into a B2B SaaS website?

SEO and AI search belong in the build, not in a later project. The work covers technical SEO, schema, semantic structure, and topical authority, plus answer engine optimization (AEO), the work of making content easy for AI assistants to read, quote, and recommend.

The same Gartner survey found that 45% of the 646 buyers used AI during a recent purchase. Google's Core Web Vitals set thresholds for a good experience: Largest Contentful Paint within 2.5 seconds, Interaction to Next Paint of 200 milliseconds or less, and Cumulative Layout Shift of 0.1 or less. Web.dev recommends meeting them at the 75th percentile of page loads (as of October 2026).

No agency controls whether a search engine or an AI assistant shows a given page, so treat any promise of a ranking or a citation with caution. ## Should you redesign, refresh, or rebuild your SaaS website?

Refresh when the structure still works and the message is out of date, redesign when the structure no longer fits how buyers decide, and rebuild when the platform or the CMS is what slows your team down. The more a project changes URLs and templates, the more it behaves like a site move, and the more search traffic it puts at risk.

Table: Refresh, redesign, or rebuild: what changes and what to protect

| Option | What changes | What to protect |
|---|---|---|
| Refresh | Messaging, visuals, and a few key pages. Structure and URLs stay. | Rankings and links on the pages that earn traffic |
| Redesign | Page structure, templates, and visual design, often on the same platform | URLs that earn traffic, tracking, and forms, with a redirect for every URL that changes |
| Rebuild | Platform, CMS structure, and content model, along with the design | Everything above, plus the accuracy of moved content |

If URLs will change, plan the work as a site move: the [guide to migrating from WordPress to Webflow](page:wordpress-to-webflow-migration) describes the method. This guide is written for marketing leads and founders at B2B SaaS and tech companies. Another option is better in these cases:

- The site is one page, and a template or a page builder is enough.
- The work is the product interface, such as in-app screens or a logged-in dashboard. This guide covers the public marketing website.
- The positioning is undecided. A new design cannot settle who the product is for.
- Nobody on your team will own the site after launch, and even an easy-to-edit site goes stale without an owner.
- Engineering requires the marketing site to live inside the product codebase. This guide assumes Webflow.

## How much does a B2B SaaS website cost, and how long does it take?

Cost and timeline depend on five things: the number of page templates, the volume of CMS content, the integrations, whether the design is new or supplied, and the number of review rounds.

Table: What sets the cost and timeline of a B2B SaaS website, and what to bring to an agency

| Driver | Why it matters | What to bring |
|---|---|---|
| Page templates | Each template is designed, built, and reviewed once, so more templates mean more of each. | A list of page types, with a count of pages for each. |
| CMS content | Every post, customer story, or integration page must be written or moved, then checked. | The number of items per type, and who supplies the content. |
| Integrations | Each connected system needs access, testing, and an owner. | The CRM, marketing automation, analytics, and consent tools the site must connect to. |
| Design | A new design takes longer than a build from a design you already have. | Brand guidelines and any existing designs. |
| Review rounds | Every round adds waiting time before the next step can start. | The names of the approvers and how fast each can respond. |

To compare quotes fairly, send every agency the same list and ask for a timeline by phase that includes the copy, assets and approvals your team owes. BenorMedia lists its own plans on its [pricing page](/pricing).

## What goes wrong in a SaaS website project, and how do you prevent it?

Four risks are worth checking before a SaaS website launches: URLs that are not redirected, a site only a developer can change, tracking added too late, and template pages that repeat the same text.

Table: Risks in a B2B SaaS website project and how to prevent them

| What goes wrong | Why it matters | How to prevent it |
|---|---|---|
| URLs change and the old ones are not redirected | Pages that earn traffic and links lose their address. Google recommends server-side permanent redirects from old URLs to new ones. | Build a URL map before the design is final, then test every redirect before launch. |
| Only a developer can change the site | Every new page and every fix waits in a queue. | Use a component library, CMS Collections, and roles that match who does what, then train the team. |
| Forms and tracking are added after launch | Leads can go missing, and there is no baseline to compare against. | Connect the CRM, analytics, and conversion tracking during the build, and test a submission end to end. |
| Template pages repeat the same text | Collections make it cheap to publish many integration or comparison pages. Google's spam policies treat many pages made mainly to manipulate rankings, not to help users, as scaled content abuse. | Give every page its own facts, use cases, and sources, and publish fewer pages. |

## What should you ask a B2B web design agency before you sign?

Before you sign with any agency, ask seven questions whose answers you can check yourself.

1. **Can we open sites you have built for companies like ours?** Visit them on a phone and a laptop, and judge speed and clarity yourself.
2. **Who does the work, and who will we talk to each week?** Ask for names and roles in writing.
3. **How will our marketers add and edit pages after launch?** Ask for a walkthrough of the roles and seats they need, and have someone on your team build a test page.
4. **What is in the scope, and what is not?** Get copywriting, design, integrations, and migration confirmed in writing.
5. **How do you protect search traffic if URLs change?** Ask to see the URL map and a redirect test before launch.
6. **What is connected before launch, and how do you test a lead end to end?** Submit a test form and confirm that it reaches your CRM.
7. **What happens after launch, and who owns the files?** Ask for the support terms, and for who holds the Webflow project, the domain and the code. BenorMedia's [ongoing website support](/ongoing-website-support) page describes its options after launch.
